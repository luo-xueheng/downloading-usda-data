         In trade year 2025/26 (Oct-Se p), global barley imports are forecast to reac           h almost                                             35 million tons,
         the second-highest volume on record after the 37 million tons recorded in 2020/21.                             Strong global
         demand for barley feed offset weaker malting demand and   supported larger shipments of
         abundant crops from major exporting countries. Imports in 2026/27 are forecast to ease to just
         over 30 million tons as  several key importers are projected to have larger domestic production and
         major exporters return to average level  crops.
         Global barley import demand has historically been generated by both feed and malting use in
         deficit countries, with a smaller portion for food and seed use. I       n recent years, feed demand has
         made up a larger portion of growth. While barley averages             85 to 90 percent of                             the value of corn in
         energy, it is often competitively  priced and meets qualitative preferences for buyers in ruminant
         feeding, such as meat or fat coloration. In contrast,   slowing growth in                                 world consumption of beer   1
         has softened demand for malted barley.

         Exporter data highlights this shift well  . In 2022/23, major exporters (Argentina, Australia, and
         Canada)                 exported about 4 million tons of barley for malting and around 9   million tons for other
         uses, largely feed. Thus far in 2025/26, these same countries exported about 5  million tons for malt
         and almost 13 million for other uses2. This surge in global import demand for feed was successfully
         met by bumper harvests across key exporters  with most major suppliers harvesting larger crops in
         2025/26, leaving them  well positioned to supply growing feed demand. In particular, expanded
         production in the EU, Canada, and Australia provided ample  and competitively priced v olume s
         necessary to fuel larger global demand.

Much of the current boom in demand for imported barley feed has come from China, which is
forecast to import  14.0 million tons of barley in 2025/26, representing more than 40 percent of
global demand. Amid a restrictive tariff rate quota  for corn, interventions to reduce corn imports,
and trade tensions affecting supplies of other feed substitutes, China has increased barley imports
by 4.0 million tons from 2024/25 to meet demand for cost effective feed by private importers.    The
other major East Asia importer is Japan, where stable demand for barley in wagyu cattle feeding
and modest malting demand sustain an average of 1.1 million tons   of imports annually.

Middle East markets also supported growth in import demand for 2025/26.         Saudi Arabia
maintained its position as the second largest importer,  rebounding to over 4.7 million tons. Saudi
buyers have long preferred using barley in mixtures with alfalfa over other                                 compound feeds to
support sheep and goat operations, though levels remain below historic peaks                     due to the removal
of import subsidies in 2019/20. Turkey, typically a marginal importer,                    substantially ramped up
imports during 2025/26 due to a historicall          y small barley crop. Libya and Jordan, static buyers who
average 1.0 million tons a year, sustained imports to support demand for affordable feed                               .

Global import demand in 2026/27 is likely to be more subdued as barley production   is forecast to
rebound in Turkey and other major  Middle East and North Africa  markets, lowe ring import needs.
Additionally, a return to smaller and more typical levels of production in all countries but Australia
will limit exportable supplies. This is compounded by  near-term limited access to maritime trade
for Russia and Ukraine, both        significant barley exporters.  China will remain the driving force for
global barley trade in 2026/27,                              with imports forecast at  13.0 million tons. Ample availability from a
record crop in Australia  and good supplies in other partners like Canada   are likely to meet this
demand.

Global trade is cut further this month with the largest   reduction                               for top exporter Russia, down 3.0
million tons to 40.0 million, which are the lowest in 5 years.                             Russian wheat exports declined
sharply during the first quarter of                              trade year 2026/27 (July 2026-June 2027)                                                compared to the same
period last year, in part                due to ongoing conflict and         trade disruptions  at Black Sea ports.
Russian                                                     exports                            have sharply declined  to the Middle East and North Africa . Port data indicates
Russian shipments to Egypt declined by nearly 50    percent and to Turkey by nearly 90 percent,
compared to the same period last year. Several m            ajor importers in these regions had favorable
growing conditions that             led to bumper crops this year, reducing the need for imports                                                 in the first
quarter. In 2026/27, record crops have been harvested  in both Turkey (23.0 million tons) and Egypt
(10.0 million tons). Stocks                           are being used to further alleviate the need for near-term imports. Egypt
imported record volumes in 2025/26 with large purchases                         in the final months. The Turkish Grain
Board recently announced it  will release 5.5 million tons of its stocks for sale into the market.

Price-sensitive importing countries across Sub  -Saharan Africa and Asia are also reacting to lower
Russia exports. Amid  increased global wheat prices, Bangladesh          and Nigeria are turning to
alternative suppliers and reducing imports.                            Nigeria is importing more EU wheat this year to offset
reduced Black Sea export availability           while Bangladesh is relying more heavily on imports of
Canadian wheat. Bangladesh is also reducing consumption of wheat, shifting to more affordable
domestic staple foods like rice.  Argentina, a competitively priced supplier, is shipping large r
volumes to Asia  –   especially Indonesia and Thailand –   and will have plentiful supplies following its
November harvest.  With high wheat prices and constrained supplies from Russia, countries across
the globe are likely to continue to diversify suppliers to meet demand.
