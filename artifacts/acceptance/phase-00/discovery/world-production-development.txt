Argentina Wheat: Favorable Conditions Boost Yield

USDA estimates Argentina wheat production for   marketing year (MY) 2026/27 at 23.0 million
metric tons, up 7 percent from last month, but    down 18 percent from last year. Production for MY
2026/27 is the second highest on record, following last     year’s record crop. Wheat yield is estimated
at 3.59 tons per hectare, up 7 pe rcent from last month, but down 12  percent from last year’s record.
Yield for MY 2026/27 is the second highest on reco rd. Harvested area is estimated at 6.4 million
hectares (mha), unchanged from last m onth, but down 6 percent from last year.

Planting occurred from the end of May until mid-A  ugust. The Argentina Ministry of Agriculture
has published a planted area of 6.6 mha. Abandonment   of wheat, outside of the outlier year of MY
2016/17, ranges from 2 to 10 percent.

Precipitation during July and Augus t for planting, emergence, and establishment of the majority
of the crop was average to above  average. September brought spottier rainfall conditions but due
to the previous month’s abundant precipitation,  soil moisture conditions  stayed above normal.

During October, the bulk of the crop went through fl  owering, which is the most critical stage of
yield development. In the main  northern wheat growing regions of Córdoba, Santa Fe, and Entre
Rios, rainfall was above normal during flowering, which boosted yields. Rainfall in the
southernmost wheat province of Buenos Aires re mained average throughout the growing season.
The above-normal vegetation conditions are reflected in the satellite-derived Sentinel-3
Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI). Harvest for the crop has just begun in the
northern provinces. Harvest will move to the ma   in wheat producing regions during November and
December. (For more information, please contact  Katie.McGaughey@usda.gov .)

Mexico Corn:  Above-Average Temperatures   and Insufficient Rainfall Reduce Crop Area

USDA estimates Mexico corn pr oduction for marketing year (MY)  2026/27 at 24.0 million metric
tons, down 500,000 metric tons, or 2 percent from  last month, 4 percent from last year, and 5
percent below the 5-year average.  Harvested area is estimated at  6.1 million hectares (mha), down
6 percent from last month, 9 per cent from last year, and  8 percent below the 5-year average. Yield
is estimated at 3.93 metric tons  per hectare (mt/ha), up 4 percent from last month, 6 percent from
last year, and 4 percent a bove the 5-year average.


=== PDF PAGE 6 ===
Mexico corn has two seasons with  the rainy spring/summer season  and the dry fall/winter season.
The majority (70 percent) of annual corn  production is from the predominantly rainfed
spring/summer season, with planting from Ap ril through August and harvest from September
through February.

Heat stress and drought caused by El Niño, during gr  ain fill, have reduced harvested area due to
abandonment as the spring/summer season harvest is underway. Back in June, sufficient rain
supported farmers in planting a simi lar area to corn as the last tw o years. However, in October,
Mexico’s Agri-Food and Fisheries Information Service (SIAP) reported that damaged or
abandoned area for the spring/summ er season has already surpasse d 650,000 hectares (ha). In
comparison, the last El Niño-caused Mexican drought  that impacted spring/summer corn was in
2023, when about 500,000 ha were damaged or abandoned, according to SIAP. The report of
abandonment from SIAP and additional reports  from farmers has resulted in a reduction to
USDA’s MY 2026/27 harvested area estimate.







































=== PDF PAGE 7 ===
While harvested area decreased, USDA estimates an  increase in yield, as the drought and above-
normal temperatures impact rainfed corn more th an irrigated corn. The spring/summer season is
typically 70 percent rainfed and 30 percent irrigated. In 2023, when El Niño induced drought
impacted spring/summer corn, the lower-yielding rainfed crop was impacted more than the higher-
yielding irrigated crop. However, this year impa cts from El Niño are more evident in southern
Mexico where the crop is primarily rainfed.

The fall/winter season corn planting has begun  and typically accounts for 30 percent of annual
production, yet is mostly (60 per cent) irrigated, resulting in higher yields. Water levels in the
reservoirs are sufficient to suppor t irrigation in Sinaloa, where mo st of the fall/winter corn is
produced. For the fall/winter corn growing seas on, planting recently began in November and
harvest is from mid-April through July. (For more information, please contact
Lisa.Colson@usda.gov.)

South Africa Corn: Lower Planting Inte ntions Reduce Production Prospects

USDA forecasts South Africa  corn production for marketing year (MY) 2026/27 at 15.0 million
metric tons, down 9 percent from last month and  the 5-year average, and down 18 percent from
last year’s record crop. The corn production forecast includ es output from both developing and
commercial sectors, with the comm  ercial sector accounting for a pproximately 96 percent of total
corn production. Harvested area is forecast at 2.8    million hectares, down 8 percent from last month
and 12 percent from last year. Yield is forecast at 5.45 metric tons  per hectare, similar to the 5-
year average and down 7 percent from last yea r’s record yield during a La Niña year.

Corn planting in South Africa typically starts  from early October and extends to mid-January,
depending on the arrival of seasonal planting rains.   If rain is delayed duri ng the optimal planting
season from October through December, sowing  can continue into mid-January. Planting was
delayed during October due to be low-average rainfall throughout South Africa’s grain belt. The
greatest rainfall deficits during October occurred in eastern Mpumalanga province, where the
optimal planting window is from Oc tober 1 through November 15.

October. It forecasted commercial corn area  planting intentions down by 337,000 hectares (12
percent) from last year while sunflowerseed area was up by 255,000 hectares (45 percent).
Producers are switching to more drought resistant   crops, such as sunflowerseed, because a very
strong El Niño drought this year is forecasted from record warm  waters measured in the central
Pacific. If corn fails to be established by the    end of the year, producers  may switch to sowing more
sunflowerseed into mid or late January. (For more information, please contact

Nicaragua Rice: Drought Devastates Ri ce Production in the Dry Corridor

USDA estimates Nicaragua rice  production for marketing year (M Y) 2026/27 at 225,000 metric
tons (milled basis), down 16 percen t from last month and 17 percent  from last year. Harvested area
is estimated at 70,000 hect ares, down 13 percent from last mont h and last year. Yield (rough basis)
is estimated at 4.87 tons per hect are, down 4 percent from last mont h and 5 percent from last year.





















Nicaragua is the largest rice producer in Centra l America and has three different rice growing
cycles. Planting of the rainfed crop for the first    (primera) cycle begins in June, with the beginning
of the rainy season. About 30 percent of the ra infed areas are in the Dry Corridor in western
Nicaragua. Drought in western Nicara  gua has devastated rice in the Dry Corridor along the Pacific
coast. The rice is about 75 percent irriga  ted in the remaining two cycles, second   (invierno) and
third  (verano). Planting for the second cycl e is typically from July through September; however,
it was completed late this year . The remaining half of the ir rigated rice crop, known as the  verano
cycle, will be planted in January and Fe  bruary and harvested from April through June.   (For more
information, please contact  Lisa.Colson@usda.gov.)

India Cotton: Production Reduced on Dryness

USDA estimates marketing year 2026/27 cotto n production for India at 23.5 million 480-pound
bales, down 2 percent from last m  onth and 1 percent from last year. Harvested area is estimated at
11.5 million hectares, unchanged from last month,  but down marginally from last year. Yield is
estimated at 445 kilograms per hectare, down 2 percent from last month and 1 percent from last
year.

India cotton is cultivated predominantly during the  kharif season, from May to October, which
accounts for roughly 98 percent of na  tional production. The major pr oducing regions for the cotton
kharif season are in the central and southern   regions, accounting for roughly 61 percent and 24
percent of the cropping season, respectively. Over  two-thirds of India cotton is cultivated under
rainfed conditions driven by the southwest mons oon, while the remainder is cultivated under
controlled irrigation.  A weak  and erratic 2026 southwest monsoon  season resulted in cumulative

=== PDF PAGE 14 ===
precipitation being 13 percent below the long-term   average of 759.4 millimeters. According to the
India Meteorological Department, this year is rank  ed the fourth lowest since 2001. Severe rainfall
deficits were observed primarily in  the east, northeast, and south.


























Early season dry periods delayed planting as  reports from the Department of Agriculture &
Farmers Welfare indicated planting progress was  15 percent behind last year. Timely rainfall
during the mid-season, however, en abled planting to progress and conclude marginally behind last
year. This season, insufficient water availability  coupled with heat stress likely impacted root
growth, lowered boll formation, and stunted  plant size, thereby reducing yield potential. Kharif
cotton is currently being harvested. (For additional information, please contact

The USDA and NASA Global Agricultural Monitoring (GLAM) system provides near real-time and science quality

https://geo.fas.usda.gov/GADAS/index.html
USDA's Foreign Agricultural Service (FAS) provides the Global Agricultural and Disaster Assessment System

earth observation data streams, particularly targeted towards agricultural and disaster assessment analysis. GADAS is
an interactive website which provides analysts with a wide variety of routine geospatial products (maps, charts, tables)
they require for comprehensive situational investigations and recurring assessments.

Export Sales Reporting
https://apps.fas.usda.gov/esrquery/
USDA’s Export Sales Reporting Program monitors U.S. agricultural export sales on a daily and weekly basis. Export
sales reporting provides a constant stream of up-to-date market information for 40 U.S. agricultural commodities sold
abroad. The weekly U.S. Export Sales report is the most currently available source of U.S. exports sales data. The data
is used to analyze overall levels of export demand, determine where markets exit, and assess the relative position of
U.S. commodities in foreign markets.

Global Agricultural Trade System (GATS)
https://apps.fas.usda.gov/gats/default.aspx
The Global Agricultural Trade System (GATS) is a searchable database containing monthly U.S. Census Bureau trade
data organized by agricultural commodity and agricultural related product groups. Trade data is searchable by partner
countries and partner groups. Historical U.S. agricultural trade data is available back to 1967. In addition, U.N. trade
statistics (UN Comtrade) may be queried through GATS. UN trad   e data is available for nearly 200 countries or areas,
dating from the inception of the Harmonized System (HS) of trade codes in 1989 to present. The database is
continuously updated. U.S. trade data is updated monthly according to the U.S. Census Bureau’s reporting system.
UN Comtrade data are updated in GATS after nationally submitted data to the UN are standardized by the UN
Statistical Division and added to the UN Comtrade database.
